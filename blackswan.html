Black Swan · phased investing

Does staging soften crash-entry risk, and what does it cost if no crash comes?

The intended policy is a 3M–2Y Treasury ladder for 24 or 36 months, followed by equal monthly stock purchases over 12 months. This stress test uses the 3-month yield as a fixed proxy; it does not simulate a full 3M–2Y ladder or future roll-down and reinvestment rates. The latest Treasury par-curve snapshot is dated 25 Nov 2026. Results are per $1,000,000 of proceeds and scale linearly. The 1929 and 2008 price-index drawdown depths are verified against Federal Reserve History; recovery lengths and post-trough paths remain model assumptions. Crash paths are stress designs, not forecasts.

Reinvestment risk, next to the headline

Staging trades crash exposure for cash yield risk.

The latest fetched official par-yield snapshot (25 Nov 2026) is 4.34% for 3-month bills and 4.61% for 2-year notes. The model uses the 3-month rate as a frozen cash-yield proxy; it is not a 3M–2Y ladder schedule or a promise of reinvestment rates. If yields reset to 2.00%, the staged results are lower. What this means for a 2040–2050 inheritance.

A plan built on one inflation rate, one equity return, or one Treasury yield across 50 years is built on a fiction. The model's designed sequences are deliberately irregular (recession, crisis, stagflation, baseline) precisely because real economic conditions shift regime. The sequence positions are rotated to test order risk, not to predict which regime arrives when.

Real conditions over 50 years

Growth and inflation are not steady, and a 50-year view must plan for regime change

A single crash is not the only failure mode. Over a 50-year holding period, the US economy has passed through double-digit inflation, double-digit interest rates, disinflation, a housing bust, and a pandemic-era inflation spike. No single sequence captures all of them; the model uses four designed sequences instead. What this means for a 50-year projection.

No regime lasts forever, and a path that works in the Great Moderation fails in the Great Inflation. The model's four designed sequences are deliberately uneven precisely because growth and inflation never stay at one level for 50 years. Staging in Treasuries protects against early-entry shocks but creates reinvestment risk when cash yields fall — the exact trade shown in the results table. A reader should treat every 50-year output as a stress test across regimes, not as a forecast of which regime will arrive.
