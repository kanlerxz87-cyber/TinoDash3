Does staging soften crash-entry risk, and what does it cost if no crash comes?

A stylised test of parking sale proceeds in 3-month to 2-year Treasuries for 24 or 36 months, then buying stocks in equal monthly steps over 12 months. Results are per $1,000,000 of proceeds and scale linearly. The crash depths are not yet verified against an index provider; reinvestment risk is shown beside every result.

What this means for a 2040–2050 inheritance.

A plan built on one inflation rate, one equity return, or one Treasury yield across 50 years is built on a fiction. The model's designed sequences are deliberately irregular (recession, crisis, stagflation, baseline) precisely because real economic conditions shift regime. The sequence positions are rotated to test order risk, not to predict which regime arrives when.

Real conditions over 50 years

Growth and inflation are not steady, and a 50-year view must plan for regime change

A single crash is not the only failure mode. Over a 50-year holding period, the US economy has passed through double-digit inflation, double-digit interest rates, disinflation, a housing bust, and a pandemic-era inflation spike. No single sequence captures all of them; the model uses four designed sequences instead. The table below records the official dating and policy context for each major regime, so a reader can see what a real 50-year path might contain.

What this means for a 50-year projection.

No regime lasts forever, and a path that works in the Great Moderation fails in the Great Inflation. The model's four designed sequences are deliberately uneven precisely because growth and inflation never stay at one level for 50 years. Staging in Treasuries protects against early-entry shocks but creates reinvestment risk when cash yields fall — the exact trade shown in the results table. A reader should treat every 50-year output as a stress test across regimes, not as a forecast of which regime will arrive.

What is not yet verified on this page

  1. The 1929-type and 2008-type index drops and recovery lengths are stylised and need checking against an index-provider series.
  2. The equity growth after recovery and the staging design are the owner's assumptions, not official figures.
  3. The results show a deterministic path, not a probability distribution. They do not capture ties between cash yields and stock crashes.
  4. The baseline anchors are official SSA and CBO projections, not forecasts. The CBO 2056 long-range debt path is only secondary-cited and is not used here.
